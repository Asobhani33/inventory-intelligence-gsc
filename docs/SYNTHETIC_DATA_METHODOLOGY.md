# Synthetic Inventory & Demand Layer — Methodology

Generated by `src/simulate_inventory.py` (run via `notebooks/02_data_engineering.ipynb`).
License: **CC0** — original work of this project, see `docs/DATA_PROVENANCE.md`.

## Why this layer exists

No public dataset audited for this project (10 candidates, Inventory
Intelligence Sourcebook Section A.3) publishes real on-hand stock levels.
Rather than reusing an unlicensed dataset (DataCo) or a dataset with no
industrial network structure, this project builds its own inventory/demand
layer **on top of the real Brunel network** — real plant/warehouse count,
capacities, handling costs, and freight lead times — and documents exactly
how, so a reviewer can audit every assumption below.

## What's real vs. generated, table by table

| Table | Real (from Brunel) | Generated |
|---|---|---|
| `Dim_Warehouse` | Plant ID, handling cost/unit, daily capacity, linked ports | Region label (see caveat below) |
| `Dim_SKU` | Which SKUs are stocked at which warehouse (`ProductsPerPlant`) | Unit cost, base annual demand, category label, ABC class |
| Lead time per warehouse | Average `TPT_Day_Count` from `FreightRates`, matched via the warehouse's linked ports | — |
| `Fact_Inventory` / `Fact_Demand` / `Fact_Orders` | Network topology the simulation runs on | Entirely generated by the simulation below |

## Assumptions that need a caveat

- **Region labels are illustrative, not real geography.** The Brunel dataset
  anonymizes plant/port codes (`PLANT01`, `PORT01`, …) with no country
  field. Regions are assigned deterministically from a hash of the plant
  code purely so the Power BI "Global Network" page has something to map —
  never claim these are the real locations.
- **Product category labels** (Wear Parts, Consumables, Spare Components,
  Filtration & Fluids, Electrical & Sensors, Fasteners & Hardware) are
  assigned the same way, to give the story an industrial-aftermarket feel
  consistent with the project's framing (a Metso-Consumables-style network).
- **Unit cost and base annual demand** are drawn from log-normal
  distributions (seeded per-SKU, so fully reproducible) chosen to produce a
  right-skewed, Pareto-like spread — a few expensive/fast-moving parts, a
  long tail of cheap/slow movers — which is what real spare-parts
  populations look like, but the specific numbers are not derived from any
  real transaction.
- **Ordering cost** ($35–$180/order) and **holding cost rate** (22%/year of
  unit value) are industry-typical placeholder constants; the real Brunel
  `WhCosts.Cost_Per_Unit` is used as the warehouse handling-cost input.

## Simulation mechanics

For each of the 2,036 real (SKU, Warehouse) pairs from `ProductsPerPlant`:

1. **Classify ABC** by ranking synthetic annual value (`unit_cost × base
   annual demand`) across all SKUs and cutting at the classic 70% / 90%
   cumulative-value thresholds.
2. **Assign demand variability** (coefficient of variation) by class —
   tighter for A (0.3–0.6), looser for C (1.1–2.3) — reflecting that
   fast-movers are typically steadier and slow-movers more intermittent.
3. **Generate daily demand** with a Negative Binomial draw (not Normal/
   Poisson) parameterized to hit the target mean and variance — this
   produces the lumpy, many-zero-days pattern real intermittent spare-parts
   demand has, which a symmetric distribution would miss entirely.
4. **Compute the replenishment policy** from classic formulas:
   `EOQ = sqrt(2 × annual demand × ordering cost / holding cost)`,
   `Safety Stock = z × σ_demand × sqrt(lead time)` (z by target service
   level: 97.5% / 95% / 90% for A/B/C), `Reorder Point = mean daily demand
   × lead time + Safety Stock`.
5. **Simulate day by day** for a 2-year window (2024-01-01 to 2025-12-31,
   chosen independently of the Brunel `OrderList` dates — only the network
   *structure* is real, not the historical time axis): receive any arriving
   orders, fulfill demand from on-hand stock, carry unmet demand as
   backorder, flag a stockout day when on-hand hits zero with demand still
   outstanding, and place a new EOQ-sized order whenever on-hand + on-order
   falls to or below the reorder point.

## What came out (this run)

- **1,487,585** Fact_Inventory rows and **1,487,585** Fact_Demand rows
  (2,035 series × 731 days), **17,119** replenishment orders.
- ABC mix: **14.4% A / 22.2% B / 63.4% C** of SKUs — a realistic Pareto
  split.
- **16%+ of series experienced at least one stockout** over the 2-year
  window in early validation runs — enough positive examples for the
  Phase 4 stockout classifier without being trivially imbalanced.
- Real freight lead times cluster at **2–3 days** for most lanes, per the
  Brunel `FreightRates` table — see `docs/phase2_validation_charts.png`.

## A known, interesting quirk — worth discussing, not hiding

In this run, **Class A** (highest-volume, tightest-CV, highest
service-level-target SKUs) shows a *slightly higher* raw stockout-day rate
than B/C, which looks backwards at first glance. The likely mechanism: EOQ
grows with `sqrt(demand)` while mean daily demand grows linearly with it, so
high-volume A items carry proportionally *fewer days of supply* per order
cycle than low-volume C items — meaning they cycle closer to zero more
often, even with a tighter service-level target. This is a legitimate
emergent property of a fixed-EOQ batch-ordering policy interacting with
negative-binomial demand, not a bug in the safety-stock formula. It's a
genuinely useful discussion point for the Phase 5 optimization notebook
("should fast movers move to a more frequent, smaller-batch review policy
instead of classic EOQ?") — flag it explicitly in the final write-up rather
than smoothing it away.

## Reproducibility

Every random draw is seeded from a SHA-256 hash of a stable business key
(SKU, warehouse, or the pair) — never a bare global seed — so re-running
`simulate_inventory.py` regenerates byte-identical data, and the numbers for
one SKU never shift just because another SKU was added or removed.
